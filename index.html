Your job and the supply chain

Who earns the honest order?

You do not have to work in supply chain to affect how it performs. The quickest way to see that is to sit on the other side of it for a few minutes.

You need 100 units a week. There is a shortage, and all three of your suppliers are short. You will place the same order with each of them. The only thing that differs is how each one has treated you until now.

Almost none of that was a supply chain decision

What actually moved your order

It was not their prices, their products or their planning software. It was whether you believed what they told you, and whether they had done what they said they would do.

  • They tell you early, including bad newsSales and account management
  • They do what they promised, on the date they promised itOperations and logistics
  • They do not quietly reprice when supply gets tightFinance and pricing
  • They say how they will share out short supplySupply chain planning

Only the last of those belongs to supply chain. By the time a shortage starts, the other three have already been decided, and none of them can be created in the week the shortage begins.

The planner inherits whatever the rest of the company spent the last two years building.

So what has it got to do with you?

  • If you talk to customers Telling them bad news early is unpleasant and it is the cheapest thing your company can do to keep their orders honest.
  • If you run operations or service Every date you keep is the reason somebody believes the next one. Every date you miss quietly is worth more than the delay itself.
  • If you set prices or budgets A price that moves when supply tightens teaches customers to buy ahead, and a stock cut made for the quarter end removes the buffer that was hiding the problem.

The point. Customers inflate their orders in proportion to how unsure they are about what they will actually get. That uncertainty is created, or removed, by people who never open an allocation spreadsheet.
